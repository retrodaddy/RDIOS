# RDIOS Institutional Policy Model v1

## Context

The founder's own words frame this precisely: Governance decided who decides. This document decides how a decision, once someone has standing to make it, should actually be made. Every institution on the list that closes this document already lives by policies — a travel policy, a purchasing policy, a child safety policy, a hospital emergency protocol — written, revised, and enforced for decades, almost always with no software involved at all. Most software that claims to support this hides the policy inside application code: a threshold hardcoded into a form's validation, an approval rule buried in a workflow definition nobody outside engineering can read. The founder's instruction reverses that completely: **policies should exist independently of software. Applications should obey policies. Never define them.**

## The one reframe everything below depends on

**A Policy is not a rule an application enforces. It is a standing institutional statement an application is obligated to consult.** The distinction sounds small and is not. A rule an application enforces lives and dies with that application — rewritten in code, readable only by whoever can read the code, silently divergent from whatever the institution's actual Board Resolution says the moment someone edits one without the other. A statement an application consults is authored once, in the institution's own words, owned by the institution, and every application that touches a policy-governed decision reads the same statement, faithfully, forever. Every question below is this same sentence, examined from a different angle.

---

## 1. What is an Institutional Policy?

Five concepts sit close together in this model, and confusing any pair of them is exactly how software ends up hiding policy inside things that were never meant to hold it. Each answers a genuinely different question:

- **Purpose** (Institution Setup Experience v2) answers *why does this institution exist at all* — one sentence, true before any person, any Position, any decision. Purpose is prior to everything else in this list; nothing below could exist without something to be in service of.
- **Governance** (Governance & Responsibility Model v1) answers *who currently has standing to decide* — Areas of Responsibility, held by seats, resolved fresh against the real organization graph.
- **Policy** answers *what must be true for that decision to be correct* — not who may approve a purchase, but what "correctly approved" actually means: a threshold, a required number of quotes, a class of vendor that's disallowed, an age below which a guardian's consent is required. Policy is the substantive content a decision is measured against, independent of who happens to be making it today.
- **Workflow** (the Work Engine, Shared Engine Layer) answers *what are the mechanical steps* — the state machine that actually moves a decision from submitted to approved to complete. A workflow can exist with almost no policy content at all (a simple two-signature approval, no substantive criteria beyond "get two signatures"); Policy is what usually gives a workflow's steps something real to check for, but the two remain genuinely separable — Workflow is process, Policy is criteria.
- **Configuration** (Institution Configuration Layer, Product Foundation §8, "Business Rules") answers *what specific parameter does an application read* — an approval threshold, a currency, a fiscal year. Configuration is what's left of a Policy after everything is stripped away except the exact numbers and flags an application needs to function. This is the boundary most worth stating precisely: **a Business Rule is the compiled, machine-readable residue of a Policy — never the Policy itself.** A Purchasing Policy might contain a paragraph of reasoning about preferring local vendors where quality is comparable, alongside the one number — ₹50,000 — that Work's purchase-request flow can actually read and act on. The number is Configuration. The paragraph, and the number's justification, and everything a human would need to understand *why* the threshold sits where it does, is the Policy. Every Business Rule that constrains a real decision should be traceable back to a Policy that justifies it; a Business Rule with no Policy behind it is exactly the "hidden inside code" failure mode this whole document exists to prevent, just moved one layer up, into configuration instead of code.

A Responsibility (Governance's Area) tells an application who is allowed to touch a decision. A Policy tells that same person, and everyone who reviews the decision later, what a correct instance of that decision actually looks like. Neither one is a substitute for the other, and neither is complete without the other — a decision made by someone with standing but no policy to measure against is arbitrary; a beautifully written policy nobody has standing to apply is decoration.

## 2. Where do policies live?

**Policies belong to the institution. Never to an application.** This is not a new principle — it is the identical logic that already places Purpose, Governance, and Configuration at the institution level rather than inside any one application's tables, applied to one more concept that clearly belongs beside them. A Purchasing Policy is not Work's policy or Money's policy; it is the institution's stance on purchasing, and both Work's purchase-request flow and Money's expense-approval flow need to read the identical statement when either of them touches a purchasing decision. If Policy lived inside an application, two failures follow immediately: the same substantive rule would need to be reinvented, slightly differently, the moment a second application needed to respect it — precisely the fragmentation this platform has spent five milestones refusing to allow — and an institution could never open one place and see everything it has decided about how it operates, which is exactly the kind of institutional memory the Product Philosophy already names as the whole reason RDIOS exists.

Policy lives at the Institution Configuration Layer, conceptually beside Business Rules — but as a richer, first-class record than a flat parameter, for reasons the naming discipline below (§"Where this leaves the current architecture") makes precise.

## 3. Policy Lifecycle

Philosophy only — no implementation, no schema, and deliberately reusing mechanism this platform has already frozen rather than inventing a parallel one for Policy specifically.

- **Created.** Drafted by whoever holds the Area of Responsibility the policy governs — a Finance-Area holder drafts a Purchasing Policy; a Governance-Area holder drafts a Board Resolution. Authorship is itself an ordinary act of Responsibility, nothing new.
- **Reviewed.** A policy names its own review cadence as part of its own content — annual, biennial, event-triggered — the same way Purpose is permanent but never demands re-confirmation on a schedule software invented. When a policy's review date arrives, that is an ordinary Act Now item, surfaced through the identical Attention Contract every other application already implements — never a separate "policy reminder" system.
- **Approved.** Uses the Approval Chain mechanism Governance §5 already froze, applied to the policy itself — a policy's approval chain is configured per policy type: a Board Resolution might require a quorum of Trustees; a Department Policy might require only the Department Head. No separate policy-approval workflow gets invented; Policy simply becomes one more thing an Approval Chain can be the chain *for*.
- **Superseded.** A new version replaces an old one, but the old version is never edited or deleted — the identical discipline the Audit Engine already applies to every record it holds ("corrections are new records, never edits"). The superseded policy stays permanently readable, marked as such, pointing to what replaced it and carrying the reason. An institution should always be able to answer "what was our Travel Policy in 2019," not only "what is it now."
- **Archived / Retired.** A policy that no longer applies — the institution stopped doing the thing it governed — is archived, never deleted. Same append-only, tenant-scoped, permanent discipline as everything else this platform already holds itself to.

Nothing above requires a new engine. It requires the Approval Chain, History, and Audit mechanisms already frozen to treat a Policy record as one more subject they already know how to handle.

## 4. Relationship with Governance

**Governance decides who decides. Policy decides what a correct decision looks like once they do.** The two are genuinely orthogonal, and a real institutional decision needs both to be legitimate. Authority without policy is arbitrary power — a Finance Manager with standing to approve anything, on a whim, with no way for the institution to say afterward that a specific approval was wrong. Policy without authority is inert prose — a carefully written Purchasing Policy nobody has standing to actually apply to a real purchase.

Using the founder's own example: an Approval Chain says *Purchase → Finance → Founder*. That's Governance — it names who is asked, in what order. Policy is what "Finance" is actually checking for at that step: is this vendor on the approved list, is the amount within threshold, does it need two competing quotes first. The chain is the skeleton an approval moves through. Policy is what each joint in that skeleton actually has to verify before it's allowed to flex.

## 5. Relationship with Applications

Work, Money, Assets, CRM, Projects, Documents, HR, Reports all inherit institutional policy through the exact same discipline Governance §11 already established for responsibility: **no application invents its own rules.** An application registers, once, which of its own decision types are policy-governed and which policy category applies — Purchasing, Travel, Leave, Asset Usage, Conflict of Interest — pure Institution Configuration Layer data, nothing more. The actual substantive content of "what the Purchasing Policy says" lives at the institution level, authored once, and every application whose decisions that policy touches reads the identical record. When Work's purchase-request flow and Money's expense-approval flow both need to check whether a decision falls inside the Purchasing Policy, they read the same institutional Policy — never two slowly-diverging copies of "the rules," one accidentally baked into each application's own code.

## 6. Relationship with Tamizhi

This is the single most important boundary in the entire document, and it extends two already-frozen principles rather than inventing a third. Institution Intelligence Principles v1 already settled that intelligence is a contributor, never a voice of its own. Governance §10 already settled that Tamizhi cannot approve, activate, delegate, or satisfy an approval step. Policy adds the specific capability the founder named directly: **Tamizhi interprets policy. It never invents policy. It never overrides policy.**

Interpreting means Tamizhi may read an actual Policy record's content — the threshold, the required conditions, the stated exceptions — and compare it against a specific pending decision, surfacing that comparison as an ordinary Be Aware or Act Now contribution attached to the decision, through the identical two doors Platform Integration Strategy already froze for it: *"This purchase exceeds the Purchasing Policy," "This leave request conflicts with the Leave Policy," "This board resolution needs a second trustee's approval."* Every one of those sentences is Tamizhi doing arithmetic and comparison against a document a human wrote — never Tamizhi deciding what the document should have said.

**What Tamizhi may never do:** silently update a Policy's content, invent a threshold no human ever approved, or override a policy's stated rule even when its own reasoning suggests the policy is outdated. Noticing that a policy might need revisiting is itself just another observation — *"this is the fourth exception request against the Travel Policy this quarter"* — that a human holding the relevant Area acts on through the ordinary Policy Lifecycle (§3), the same as any other institutional decision. Tamizhi may make an institution aware its policy might be stale. Tamizhi may never make itself the reason the policy changed.

## 7. Policy Hierarchy

Institution Constitution → Board Resolution → Department Policy → Temporary Emergency Policy. **Yes, hierarchy exists**, and it uses the same intuition the organization graph already proved works for authority (Governance §2, §7): just as Positions can report upward, a Policy can name which other Policy it is subordinate to — or name none, if it is foundational. A subordinate policy may narrow or add constraints on top of what a superior policy already requires. It may never loosen or contradict what the superior policy states. A Department Policy that quietly permits what a Board Resolution forbids is not a local variation; it's a conflict, and the correct behavior is to surface that conflict as an Act Now item for whoever holds the Area responsible for reconciling it — never something software resolves silently by picking a side.

Emergency Policy is the one deliberate, bounded exception, and it reuses Governance §9's own mechanism precisely: a pre-declared, time-boxed policy that is allowed to temporarily override a standing one, activated only by the same pre-named Area Emergency Governance already requires, expiring automatically, after which the standing policy underneath resumes exactly as it was, unedited.

This isn't necessarily one single tree per institution — a multinational NGO might run a Global Policy above several Country-Level Policies; a temple might have a founding trust deed sitting above everything else it has ever written. The rule generalizes regardless of shape: **narrow freely, downward. Never contradict, upward.**

## 8. Institution Types

**Every institution inherits the identical Policy model. No institution is handed identical policies.** This is the exact "same mechanism, different content" answer Institution Setup Experience v2 already gave for Organization Templates, extended to Policy without needing a new argument: a hospital and a temple both get Created / Reviewed / Approved / Superseded / Archived, the same relationship to Governance, the same hierarchy rule — and then author entirely different content, often starting from a per-institution-type starter set the same way a hospital's org chart template looks nothing like a temple's. No policy content is ever hardcoded into RDIOS. A Child Safety Policy is not a feature RDIOS ships; it is a Policy record any institution that needs one authors, using the identical mechanism a factory uses for its Safety Policy or a church uses for its own governance statement.

## 9. Human Judgment

Policy narrows the space a person has to actively deliberate over — it pre-decides the common, well-understood, low-stakes cases (*expenses under ₹5,000 need no second approval*) so that real judgment is spent where it actually matters: the genuinely ambiguous or high-stakes case a policy's authors never fully anticipated, because no policy ever can. A policy written to eliminate judgment entirely is a brittle policy — it will meet a case it didn't foresee, and when it does, an institution needs a way to bend without breaking the rule for everyone else.

That way is the **exception path**, and a policy without one is a worse policy, not a stricter one: a named Area of Responsibility may explicitly approve a deviation from a policy, with a recorded reason, and that exception is itself an ordinary decision — governed, auditable, never a quiet workaround. The exception path is where an institution's judgment stays visibly alive inside a policy-governed system, and — closing the loop back to §6 — a pattern of exceptions is exactly the signal that eventually tells the institution its policy, not its people, is the thing that needs to change.

## 10. Future-Proofing

Companies. Hospitals. Schools. Universities. Temples. Mosques. Churches. Governments. Factories. Political parties. NGOs. Family offices. Every one of them already has, right now, real prose documents — a Travel Policy, a Purchasing Policy, a Hiring Policy, a Child Safety Policy, a Hospital Emergency Protocol, a Temple Ritual Policy, a Church Governance Statement, an NGO Grant Policy, a Factory Safety Policy — written, reviewed, and revised over years or decades, almost entirely without software. This model was not built by imagining what those documents might someday contain and encoding it. It was built by naming the shape every one of them already has, regardless of content: a standing statement, owned by the institution, with a lifecycle, sitting in a hierarchy, applied by whoever currently holds standing to decide, interpreted but never authored by intelligence, and always leaving room for a human to say "this is the exception, and here's why." The model survives every institution on that list for the same reason Governance's own model did — it describes how institutions already govern themselves, in their own words, rather than asking them to adopt a vocabulary invented for software.

## Where this leaves the current architecture — one genuine gap, named precisely, not a flaw

Nothing built in M1 through M5 is wrong, and nothing here asks any of it to change shape. The honest finding, having read this philosophy against what actually exists: **Policy, as a first-class institutional record — prose, an owner, a lifecycle, a hierarchy position, a review cadence — has no home yet.** Only its narrow numeric shadow does. Product Foundation §8 already names "Business Rules — approval thresholds, currency, fiscal year, working hours — configuration values the Shared Engine Layer reads, never hardcodes," and that description is correct as far as it goes — but a Business Rule, as currently named, is a flat parameter with nowhere to hold the reasoning, the version history, or the exception path that make a real Policy more than a number.

This is not a flaw in what exists. It is a genuine, previously-unnamed domain concept — exactly the kind of gap the founder's own Build → Verify → Refine directive was written to catch, surfaced here before Work makes the absence expensive rather than after. **The smallest possible correction, for whenever implementation resumes:** Business Rules should be understood, and eventually built, as the compiled, machine-readable output *of* a Policy record — not as a replacement for one. A Policy is the human-readable statement, versioned and governed exactly as §3 describes; its Business Rules are the specific parameters extracted from it that an application actually reads. Nothing about the Institution Configuration Layer's placement, the Shared Engine Layer's role, or any table shape already built needs to change to make room for this — Policy simply becomes a new record type sitting where Business Rules already sit conceptually, with Business Rules becoming a view into it rather than the whole of it.

## What this means, practically, for Work and every application after it

Work's purchase-request flow does not invent its own idea of "what makes a purchase valid" — it consumes the institution's Purchasing Policy, unchanged, through the same Approval Chain and Business Rules mechanisms already frozen. Money's expense-approval flow reads the identical Policy, never a second, quietly different copy of the same rule. Every future application's first design conversation about "what's allowed here" starts from a Policy record the institution already owns, never from a blank page inside that application's own code.

## The closing test

**If every future application inherited this policy model unchanged, would RDIOS still feel like one operating system instead of many separate applications?**

Yes. The alternative — each application quietly deciding for itself what counts as a valid purchase, a valid leave request, a valid board resolution — is precisely the failure this whole engagement has spent five milestones refusing to allow, restated one layer higher. An institution's rules live in one place, owned by the institution, written in its own words, applied identically no matter which application a decision happens to pass through, interpreted but never authored by Tamizhi, and always leaving a named door open for a human to say *this is the exception*. Nothing in this model is phrased in the vocabulary of software — a policy, a review, a supersession, an exception — because none of it needed to be. Institutions have been writing policies since long before there was software to hide them inside, and the model that lets RDIOS remember them faithfully is simply the one that never tried to replace them.
