Status: 🟡 Constitutional design review — design-only. No code, no schema, no roadmap changes, no modification to any existing implementation. Extends the RDIOS Product Foundation v1, Audit Engine Design v1, Governance & Responsibility Model v1, Institutional Policy Model v1, and Institution Intelligence Principles v1. Contradicts none of them — every conclusion below was checked against each before being written, and where a tempting conclusion would have required reopening one of them, it was rejected instead.

# ARUMBU Universal Record Model v1

## Method

The instruction was to try to disprove this, not to arrive already believing it. Each question below was worked through by first asking what would have to be true for the answer to be "no" — for History, Tasks, Assets, Contacts, and Policies to genuinely be different species with nothing real in common beyond a passing resemblance — and only accepting "yes, they're one thing" where that attempt at disproof actually failed. In several places it did not fail cleanly, and those places are named honestly as rejections or as bounded exceptions, not smoothed over for the sake of a tidier document.

One piece of evidence shaped this investigation before a single question was answered, and is worth stating up front rather than burying: **this pattern has already emerged three times, independently, in documents that never referenced each other while being written.** The Audit Engine Design gives every audit record a `subject_type`/`subject_id` pair and calls it "the same polymorphic pattern already proven twice" — meaning a generic reference-to-any-record-type shape was already load-bearing infrastructure before this document existed. The Institutional Policy Model, arguing for something entirely different (why Policy needs its own home), independently concluded a Policy needs "an owner, a lifecycle, a hierarchy position, a review cadence" — Record-shaped language, arrived at for Record-shaped reasons, without the word "Record" ever being used. The Governance & Responsibility Model's Delegation and Approval Chain are both identifiable, persistent, stateful things with a beginning and an end, built to solve an authority problem, that happen to have the same shape as everything else on this document's list. Three domains, three different problems, one shape, found three times without anyone looking for it. That is a genuinely different kind of evidence than noticing after the fact that six things could theoretically be unified — it is convergent, not constructed, and it is the reason this document treats the question as worth real investigation rather than dismissing it as symmetry-seeking on sight.

## Question 1 — What is a Record?

Forgetting software entirely: an institution needs to remember specific, individual things — not "a task," but this task; not "a vendor," but this vendor — and be able to ask, later, what state that specific thing is in right now, distinct from what it used to be and distinct from every other thing of its kind. That need — to point at one particular thing and ask "what is true of this, currently" — is prior to any database, because institutions have needed it since long before software existed to answer it (a temple's donation ledger, a hospital's patient chart, a company's vendor file — all of these are the identical need, answered with paper, centuries before a schema could).

**A Record is a discrete, identifiable thing or fact, scoped to one institution, that has a state which can be asked about as of now and that may change over its lifetime.**

Three properties fall out of that definition as genuinely necessary, not chosen for convenience:

- **Identity** — distinguishable from every other Record of its kind, referenceable independently of anyone's description of it. Without this, there is nothing to point at, and "record" stops meaning anything.
- **Institution-scoping** — belongs to exactly one tenant. This is not a design choice this document is making; it is already the Tenant Architecture's own non-negotiable boundary (Product Foundation §3), restated here only because it turns out to be a genuine, necessary property of what a Record *is*, not merely a rule about where it's stored.
- **A "now"** — the property that does the most work, and the one worth stating precisely: a Record's state can be asked about at this moment, and that answer is allowed to differ from what it would have been a moment ago. This is what separates a Record from something else entirely — see Question 2.

One deliberate omission, worth naming rather than leaving implicit: this definition does not require an owner, a permission model, or any application-specific behavior. Those are real and important — see Question 3 — but they are not what makes something a Record. A Record is the smallest true thing underneath all of that: an identifiable, institution-scoped, currently-askable-about fact.

## Question 2 — Are all ARUMBU domains actually creating Records? Or something genuinely different?

Tested against the founder's own full list, deciding each on its merits rather than assuming the answer:

**Genuinely Records**, by the Question 1 definition — identity, institution-scoping, a "now" that can change: Person, Contact, Position, Task, Approval, Transaction (Expense/Income), Asset, Project, Document (the file or agreement itself — a specific signed thing, not a description of one), and, confirmed independently by the Institutional Policy Model's own language before this document existed, Policy. Delegation and Approval Chain, from the Governance Model, also qualify — a Delegation has identity, belongs to one institution, and has a state (active, ended) that changes. This is a fourth independent confirmation of the same shape, found by testing a domain this document didn't set out to test.

**Genuinely not Records — a different species, on purpose, not by oversight:**

**A History or Audit entry is not a Record.** This is the sharpest test case, and it survives scrutiny cleanly: a Record's defining property is a "now" that can differ from a moment ago. An audit record's defining property, stated explicitly and correctly in the Audit Engine Design, is the opposite — "corrections are new records, never edits... an audit trail that can be edited after the fact isn't a trail, it's a draft." An audit entry does not have a "now"; it has a "then," permanently. Trying to unify these would require either making Records immutable (which breaks the entire reason Task, Contact, and Asset exist — they need to change) or making audit entries mutable (which the Audit Engine Design explicitly and correctly forbids). This is not a semantic quibble — it is the one place in this investigation where the two candidate species have genuinely, structurally incompatible requirements, and the correct conclusion is that they are not the same thing, exactly as the Audit Engine Design already, unknowingly, built them to be. (One terminology note to avoid confusing this document with the one it's extending: Audit Engine Design's own prose calls a correction "a new audit record" — using "record" in the ordinary English sense of a database row. That is not a claim about the Universal Record Model's Record; the two uses of the word are unrelated, and this document capitalizes Record throughout specifically to keep that distinction visible.)

**A Report's rendered output is not a Record; a saved Report definition is.** A live report — the actual numbers on screen right now — is a computation over other Records, recomputed on demand, with no identity of its own independent of the moment it was run. This is the same boundary the Audit Engine Design already drew for a different case ("read/view events are never audit-worthy... looking at a record is not an institutional act worth remembering"). A saved, named report configuration someone can reopen and re-run, by contrast, has identity, belongs to one institution, and its definition can change over time — that is a Record, by the same test as everything else on the list.

**One genuine boundary case, worth naming honestly rather than forcing a clean answer: Institution.** Institution has identity, has a lifecycle, and appears throughout History — record-shaped by every test above. But it does not sit at the same altitude as everything else on this list, because Institution is the boundary every other Record is scoped *inside*, not a peer occupant of that boundary. Its "Timeline" is not a filtered slice of History the way a Contact's or a Task's is — it *is* History, undifferentiated, because everything that happens, happens to it. This is not a failure of the model; it is the correct, honest consequence of Institution being the container rather than a contained thing, and it is named here so a future reader doesn't mistake the omission for an oversight.

**Verdict: yes, genuinely, for the large majority of the list — with two real exceptions (History/Audit entries, a live Report's output) that are correctly excluded for structural reasons, not convenience, and one honest boundary case (Institution) that partially qualifies at a different altitude.** The disproof attempt failed to find a case where two things that looked alike were actually forced together — it found, instead, two places where things that don't belong together were already, correctly, kept apart by frozen decisions, and this document is simply the first place that's said so explicitly.

## Question 3 — What characteristics are truly universal?

Testing the founder's own list against a real bar: not "would this be nice for most Records" but "is this true of every Record by virtue of being a Record, or would some institutions and some Record types legitimately want it to not exist at all" — the same reusable-vs-configuration test the Platform Integration Strategy already established (§7: "if two different institution types would reasonably want this to behave identically, it's platform capability").

**Structurally mandatory — a thing isn't a Record without these:**
- **Identity** and **a "now"** — already established in Question 1, restated here only because they anchor everything else.
- **Provenance** — who or what created this Record, and when. A fact, not a grant of authority.

**Rejected, deliberately, and explained:**

- **Ownership**, as named, is rejected outright, not softened into Provenance by accident. "Ownership" implies responsibility — who answers for this Record — and that question already has a correct, frozen answer that does not belong on the Record itself: Governance & Responsibility Model §2 states plainly that "authority is never a property of a person independent of any seat," and a Record carrying its own `owner` field would quietly become a second, competing answer to a question Governance already settled through Areas of Responsibility resolved against the org graph. This isn't a small naming preference — building "Ownership" onto every Record would be building exactly the shadow authority system that document exists to prevent. Provenance survives because it only claims a fact ("this is who created it"); Ownership is rejected because it would claim authority, and Records don't get to have their own.
- **Tags** are rejected. Every domain reviewed so far already has a disciplined, institution-configured categorization mechanism — Type, in Community's model; Category, in Finance's — seeded per institution type, never hardcoded, exactly the Institution Configuration Layer's own promise. A second, unstructured, freeform tagging system sitting on top of every Record would not complement that discipline, it would compete with it: two parallel vocabularies for the same underlying need, one governed and one not, which is the identical failure mode the Platform Integration Strategy names for hardcoded type branches — "a bug report, not a shipped feature" — just relocated from code into data. The real need Tags usually serves is already met, correctly, by the Type mechanism every domain already has.
- **Versioning** is rejected as a universal property, deliberately, even though it is real and necessary for one specific Record type. The Institutional Policy Model requires it explicitly — a Policy must be supersedable, with the old version permanently readable. But Task, Contact, and Asset are correctly designed around simple mutable current-state precisely *because* most Records don't need "what did this used to say" to matter as much as "what is this now." Forcing versioning onto every Record type would be symmetry purchased at the cost of real complexity nobody asked for — the founder's own standard ("I care about architectural truth, not symmetry") rejects this directly. Versioning belongs to the specific Record types whose institutional purpose requires it, decided per type, never assumed universally.
- **Audit**, as a separate item from History, is not rejected so much as folded in — treating it as a second universal characteristic beside History would double-count one mechanism. The Audit Engine Design is explicit that History *is* the Audit Engine, read; there is nothing left over once History's answer (Question 5) is given.

**Universal doors — every Record type is automatically eligible to use these without earning them individually, but a given Record's actual use of any one of them is optional, instance-by-instance, and most Records will use most of them lightly or never — exactly the "calm is a feature" discipline the Attention Engine already lives by:**
- **Relationships** (Question 4)
- **History eligibility** — every Record is automatically a valid subject for the existing Audit Engine (Question 5)
- **Attention eligibility** — every Record *type* may register a contribution rule; most individual Records, most of the time, correctly contribute nothing (Question 6)
- **Search eligibility** (Question 8)
- **Notification eligibility** — via the same Events mechanism Notifications already listens to, using the identical "auditable classification, decided per event type" pattern the Audit Engine already established, applied here as "notification-worthy classification" rather than a new mechanism
- **Tamizhi observation eligibility** — governed entirely, unchanged, by the Institution Intelligence Principles (see the dedicated section below)
- **Document attachability** — the `DocumentRef` pattern the Finance & Assets build already proved, available to any Record type, used by however many actually need it
- **Permissions answerability** — every Record must be able to resolve "who may see or act on this," but the mechanism is not new: it is Governance's existing Areas of Responsibility, exactly the same resolution every application already performs, not a parallel permission system attached to the Record itself.
- **Comments** — included here, but honestly the weakest-evidenced entry on this list: real value for Work (already built) and plausible value elsewhere, but the case for it being structurally required, the way Identity is, was not found. Kept as a universal door rather than promoted to mandatory or demoted to rejected, because the disproof attempt neither broke it nor strengthened it.

## Question 4 — Can every Record participate in relationships? Are relationships themselves universal, or does each application keep inventing its own?

The raw material for "yes" already exists, scattered: `Asset.acquiredViaExpenseId` (Finance & Assets, a Record pointing at a Record of a different type), the Community Domain Review's named future seams (`Expense.payee` → a Contact, a Work Item's future `relatedContactId`), and — again — the Audit Engine's own `subject_type`/`subject_id`, a generic reference-to-any-record-type mechanism already trusted in production-shaped design three times over. The capability clearly wants to exist.

The real question is whether it should exist as one universal, generic relationship mechanism, or stay exactly as it is today — a new, bespoke, named field invented independently every time two Record types need to point at each other.

**The disproof attempt here actually found something.** A fully generic relationship table — `{fromType, fromId, toType, toId, kind}` — sacrifices real things a named field like `acquiredViaExpenseId` has for free: self-documentation (the field name tells a reader what the link means without looking anything up), straightforward indexing and query performance, and a guardrail against meaningless connections (nothing stops a fully generic mechanism from linking a Position to a History entry for no reason anyone could explain later). At the "millions of records" scale Question 10 asks about, a single table that every relationship in the entire institution funnels through is also a real, concrete performance risk, not a hypothetical one.

**The resolution that survives both directions of pressure:** relationships are universal as a *capability* — every Record type is eligible to participate — but the Universal Record Model does not mandate that every relationship, present or future, be expressed through one generic mechanism. Named, typed fields remain the right choice for the small number of high-frequency, structurally important, well-understood connections a domain already knows it needs — `acquiredViaExpenseId` stays exactly as it is, and a future `relatedContactId` should be built the same way, not replaced by something more generic. A universal relationship concept earns its place specifically for the long tail: the connections no single domain owns the need to invent a field for in advance (a Document supporting a Policy, two Community Contacts turning out to be the same organization under different names, a Project touching three Contacts in three different capacities) — exactly the "platform capability with a configuration surface hiding inside it" pattern the Platform Integration Strategy already names, applied here rather than invented fresh.

**Verdict: yes, universal as a capability — no, not as a mandate to collapse existing or future named fields into one generic table.** Both named fields and a generic long-tail mechanism can be simultaneously, permanently correct, serving genuinely different cases, and choosing between them per situation is a judgment call this document deliberately leaves to whoever designs the specific relationship, not a rule it tries to settle in advance.

## Question 5 — How should History interact with Records? Does History belong to every Record, or do Records merely emit events?

This one is not open — the frozen answer already exists, and the founder's own instruction ("History is already frozen elsewhere. Do not contradict it") is easy to honor because there is nothing here that pulls against it. The Audit Engine Design states plainly: "History is a read surface over `audit_records`... nothing about History is a separate data store; it is the Audit Engine, read." Records do not own History as a property. **Records emit events, exactly as every domain already does; the Audit Engine listens and writes append-only audit records keyed by `subject_type`/`subject_id`; History is the filtered read view over that.**

What the Universal Record Model actually contributes here is smaller and more useful than a new mechanism: **every Record type, purely by virtue of being a Record, is automatically a valid `subject_type`** — nothing about being a Record grants special History treatment beyond what any application's records already get today (Expense, Position, and Work Item all already prove this works). This closes the loop on the Community Domain Reconsideration's own six-month-test finding — a Contact's detail view reading as its own filtered slice of History — by confirming that finding wasn't specific to Contact at all. It generalizes to every Record, for free, because History was already built to work this way.

## Question 6 — How should Attention interact with Records? Can every Record contribute, or only some?

Testing the tempting "yes, of course, every Record could theoretically contribute" answer against the Attention Engine's own existing discipline breaks it immediately. Does a Document, sitting there, referenced, need a decision? No — its *absence* might, which is a fact about a different Record (the Expense missing it), not about the Document itself. Does a History entry need a decision? No, and it isn't a Record at all (Question 2). Does an Institution, as a Record, need a decision? Not directly — what actually surfaces (an unset Purpose, an unseated founder) is always really about some other Record underneath it.

**Not every Record contributes to Attention, and treating "could theoretically compute something" as sufficient would directly repeat a mistake this platform has already caught and corrected once** — the M7 Finance build explicitly rejected a manufactured "large expense" Act Now nudge specifically because nothing real backed it, and the Platform Cohesion Review separately found that Finance's disciplined "real decisions only" instinct was never applied retroactively to People or Work, leaving genuine gaps (a vacant position, an unassigned task) that a less disciplined "every Record can contribute" rule would have manufactured symmetrically for the wrong reason, in the wrong places, without closing the actual gap.

**Verdict: Attention eligibility is universal as a capability every Record *type* may register; actual contribution stays exactly as disciplined as it already is — real, present, true, tied to an actual decision, or it says nothing at all.** The Universal Record Model's only real contribution here is making that discipline something every future Record type inherits automatically, the same checklist item every time, rather than something each new domain has to remember to hold itself to.

## Question 7 — Tamizhi: does it reason over applications, or over Records? Would a Record model make intelligence significantly simpler?

Neither of Tamizhi's two existing doors — Search results, Attention Contract contributions — is application-shaped today. A search result is a specific Contact or a specific Task, never "something in Work." An Attention card is about one specific pending decision, never "Finance has stuff to look at." **Tamizhi already, implicitly, reasons over Records, one at a time, exactly as a colleague would — this document is naming what's already true, not proposing a change.**

Would a named Record model make this *significantly* simpler? Tested honestly: nothing about Tamizhi's capability changes either way — the Institution Intelligence Principles govern it completely, unchanged, regardless of whether "Record" is ever named as a concept. What a Record model would plausibly reduce is *implementation* duplication over time: the Audit Engine already requires every audit entry to carry a `summary` — "a human-readable sentence, rendered once, in the Assistant Voice" — and that is, unnoticed until now, exactly the shape a shared "describe this Record in plain language" capability would need. Today, Search's result renderer, Attention's card renderer, and History's summary are three independently-built things per application; a Record's canonical summary could, in principle, feed all three from one place instead of three.

**Verdict: real, but modest — an implementation-efficiency finding, not evidence that Tamizhi is currently broken or blocked without it.** Every application today successfully builds its own Search-result and Attention-card rendering without a shared Record concept existing. The honest conclusion is that a Record model would reduce *future* duplication, not that its absence has caused *present* harm.

## Question 8 — Should Search search applications, or Records? Would Universal Records create Universal Search?

Directly following from Question 7: the Platform Integration Strategy already describes Search as a provider-registry pattern where "a more capable provider registers the same way every other one does" — provider-per-capability, not provider-per-application. A search result is already, in spirit, a Record, not an application. **Universal Records don't invent Universal Search — they would make the search-result side of every future domain cheaper to build correctly**, for the identical reason named in Question 7: one shared Identity-plus-summary shape means one shared rendering path instead of one per application. Same finding, same honest limit: valuable, real, not something currently broken.

## Question 9 — Should every Record tell a story? Does every Record naturally have a Timeline?

Following directly from Question 5's answer: a Record's "Timeline" is nothing more than its own filtered slice of History, and every Record is automatically eligible for that the moment it's a valid `subject_type` — which Question 2 already established is true of nearly the entire list. A brand-new Contact with zero events yet has an empty Timeline, which is correct and unremarkable, the same way a freshly created institution's History starts with exactly one line and grows from there.

**Yes, every genuine Record naturally has a Timeline — because it's the same capability as Question 5, viewed from one Record's own detail screen instead of the institution's shared History destination, not a second thing to design.** This is exactly the finding the Community Domain Reconsideration already reached specifically for Contact ("a Contact's detail view should read as its own filtered slice of History"); this document's contribution is confirming that finding was never Contact-specific — it generalizes to every Record, automatically, because the underlying mechanism it depends on already does. The one honest exception is Institution, named in Question 2: its Timeline and History are the same thing, not two things, which is the correct consequence of it being the container rather than a contained Record.

## Question 10 — The six-year test

Hundreds of modules, millions of records — would discovering Universal Records today make that system dramatically simpler, or would it be unnecessary abstraction? This is where honesty matters most, because both a reflexive "yes, obviously" and a reflexive "no, keep it simple" are cheap answers, and neither survived real pressure.

**The case for "dramatically simpler" is not hypothetical — it's already visible at six domains, not hundreds.** The Platform Cohesion Review, months before this document, found real technical debt that is exactly this failure pattern at small scale: six independent `globalThis` store singletons each hand-rolling the identical five-line "survive dev-mode reload" guard; `notResponsible()` reimplemented byte-for-byte identically three separate times because nothing shared it; same-actor exclusion logic reimplemented twice, independently, with no shared source keeping the two copies honest. None of that happened because anyone was careless — it happened because every new domain correctly re-derived the same small decisions from scratch, and small, repeated re-derivation is exactly what silently compounds into real cost by the time a platform has hundreds of domains instead of six. A named Universal Record Model is the checklist that would have caught every one of those three specific, already-observed problems the first time, the same way Governance's Areas of Responsibility already stops every future application from re-inventing its own approval logic.

**The case against is equally real, not a strawman.** A Universal Record Model built as a single literal database table, or a single class every domain is forced to inherit from, would be precisely the kind of premature, heavy abstraction this entire engagement has correctly refused every other time it's come up — Capability staying single-institution-scoped rather than building speculative cross-tenant credentials, tamper-evidence named but not built into Audit, cross-tenant Contact matching explicitly rejected in the Community Reconsideration. Six real domains exist today. Committing to one generic table or class hierarchy now, on the strength of six domains and a hypothetical hundred more, would be exactly that mistake, repeated at the platform's most foundational layer — the one place a wrong bet would be most expensive to unwind later.

**Both are true, and they resolve the same way the Institutional Policy Model resolved an almost identical tension for Business Rules versus Policy: as a discipline layered above the existing implementation, not a replacement for it.** Business Rules didn't need replacing to make room for Policy — Policy simply named what Business Rules were always the compiled residue *of*, with nothing about any existing table needing to change. The honest answer here is the same shape: Universal Records, understood as a shared vocabulary and a shared checklist — Identity, a "now," Provenance, and automatic eligibility for History, Attention, Search, Documents, Notifications, and Tamizhi — makes the six-year system dramatically simpler, specifically *because* it never asks any existing or future Record type to give up its own typed shape, its own table, or its own named fields. It asks every new domain to answer the same ten questions this document just answered, once, instead of re-deriving them, imperfectly, one domain at a time, for the next twenty years.

## Tamizhi

No new principle. Every conclusion above either restates something the Institution Intelligence Principles already require, or extends the existing rule to a surface that didn't have a name before this document. Confirmed directly:

Tamizhi continues to observe Records the same way it already observes applications' data today — through Search and the Attention Contract, the only two doors it has ever had. It never merges, versions, relates, or reclassifies a Record on its own authority — Question 4's relationship capability and Question 3's rejected Versioning both matter here specifically because, had either been accepted without this constraint, they would have created a new *shape* of action ("auto-link these two Records," "auto-supersede this version") that Institution Intelligence Principles §5 and §6 already forbid Tamizhi from taking on its own authority, restated in a form that didn't exist before this document. It never decides — a Record's Timeline (Question 9) is exactly the kind of surface Tamizhi could eventually annotate, read-only, the same way it may already comment on a Contact's relationship going quiet, never write to it. If Tamizhi vanished tonight, per the Principles' own closing test, every Record, every Timeline, every Attention contribution must still make complete sense tomorrow morning — nothing in this document asks that test to be re-taken; it simply applies to more things than it used to have a name for.

## Where this leaves the Product Foundation

The Product Foundation already, unknowingly, names the concept this document investigates: §4 states that "every application exposes two things, and only two things, to the rest of the platform: a record surface... and an Attention Contract implementation." The word "record" was already load-bearing before this document existed — it was simply never asked what a record structurally is, or whether every application's records shared anything beyond the name.

**Universal Records are real, and they belong exactly where that sentence already sits — as a discipline the Application Layer's "record surface" requirement is checked against, not as a sixth layer, not as a new engine in the Shared Engine Layer, and not as a schema anything currently built needs to change to satisfy.** Every application still owns its own tables, its own typed shapes, its own domain logic completely, exactly as §4 already states. What changes is that "a record surface" stops being an unexamined phrase and becomes a checklist: does this new domain's central thing have Identity, institution-scoping, and a "now"? Is it a valid History subject? Could it, honestly, ever need a decision, or does it stay silent? Can it be searched, related to, attached to a document, watched by Tamizhi, through the same doors everything else already uses? A new application answers these once, the same way it already answers Governance's "which Areas do my actions belong to" and Policy's "which policy category applies" — inheriting a settled question instead of re-deriving it.

This is not a sixth layer because it doesn't coordinate anything the way the Operating System Layer does, and it doesn't provide institution-agnostic machinery the way the Shared Engine Layer does — it is a *property every Application Layer citizen is checked against*, the same altitude as the Attention Contract itself, sitting beside it rather than above or below it.

## What this settles, concretely, for whenever implementation resumes

- A Record is a discrete, identifiable, institution-scoped thing whose state can be asked about now and may change over its lifetime — three necessary properties: Identity, institution-scoping, a "now."
- History/Audit entries and a live Report's rendered output are not Records — a different, correctly separate species, already built that way by frozen decisions this document only makes explicit.
- Ownership, Tags, and universal Versioning are explicitly rejected as universal Record properties, each for a specific, named reason — not omitted by oversight.
- Relationships, History-eligibility, Attention-eligibility, Search-eligibility, Notification-eligibility, Tamizhi-observation-eligibility, Document-attachability, and Permissions-answerability are universal *capabilities* every Record type is automatically eligible for — never mandates that every Record must actively use all of them, and never a replacement for named, typed fields where those already carry real meaning.
- Nothing about History, Governance, Policy, or Institution Intelligence Principles changes shape — every connection point above extends an existing, frozen mechanism rather than inventing a parallel one.
- This settles as a discipline and a checklist sitting beside the Application Layer's existing "record surface" requirement (Product Foundation §4) — not a sixth layer, not a new engine, not a reason to touch any table that exists today.

## What remains open

- The exact canonical "describe this Record in one plain sentence" capability named in Questions 7 and 8 — real, plausible, and would reduce real duplication, but its shape (a field every Record type supplies, a function every domain implements, something else) is an implementation question for whenever Search or a second intelligence-touched application actually needs it, not something to design speculatively here.
- Where the line sits between "this deserves a named, typed relationship field" and "this belongs in a generic, long-tail relationship mechanism" (Question 4) is a judgment call this document deliberately leaves open, case by case, rather than resolving with a rule that would only be guessing at cases that don't exist yet.
- Whether Comments genuinely belongs among the universal doors or should be demoted to application-specific was the one item this investigation could neither confirm nor reject with real conviction — named honestly as unresolved rather than forced either direction.
